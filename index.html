How binaural beats actually work

Your left ear gets one pure sine tone and your right ear gets another. They are never mixed in software or in the air — with headphones on, each tone reaches exactly one eardrum. What you "hear" as a pulsing beat is created further upstream: the superior olivary complex in your brainstem compares the timing of the two signals and registers the mismatch. The rate it reports back is simply |fleft − fright|.

Two sines summed arithmetically give 2·cos(π·Δf·t) · sin(2π·favg·t): a fast tone at the average pitch, wrapped in a slow loudness envelope at the difference. The wave on the canvas above is that slow term — one crest per beat, one second of time across the width, so counting crests gives you the frequency. It is also exactly what you hear if you put both tones in the same ear, which is an ordinary amplitude beat, not a binaural one.

A word of honesty: binaural beats are a real, well-documented auditory phenomenon, but claims that they reliably "entrain" your brainwaves into a chosen band are supported mostly by small studies with mixed results. The relaxation people report may come as much from sitting still with headphones on for twenty minutes as from the beat rate. Treat the frequencies as a mood-setting dial, not a medical one.

Keep the volume moderate, don't fall asleep with headphones at high level, and skip this if you have a history of seizures or tinnitus — as with any audio, you are the best judge of what your ears can take.

Audio diagnostics

If you ever hear crackle or scratching, this tells you where it comes from. Buffer underruns above 0% mean the browser missed a deadline — usually because the machine is busy, and a bigger buffer fixes it. Underruns at 0% while you still hear noise means the page is producing a clean signal and the problem is outside it: the headphones, a Bluetooth link, or another app holding the audio device.
